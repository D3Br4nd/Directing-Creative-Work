# Average defaults

The first version a language model tends to produce, by medium. Treat each item as the floor. It is not forbidden in every context, but it must never appear by reflex. If one stays in the piece, it stays for a reason you can state.

## Writing in general

- Openers that set the scene from orbit: "In today's fast-paced world", "In an era of", "Imagine a world".
- Lists of exactly three adjectives or three parallel clauses.
- The "It's not X, it's Y" turn, used as a reflex.
- Inflated verbs: unlock, elevate, empower, delve, harness, navigate, foster, leverage.
- Abstract nouns where an object would do: journey, landscape, tapestry, realm, synergy.
- Closing lines that summarise what the reader just read, or end on a rhetorical question.
- Every sentence the same length; every paragraph the same shape.
- Hedging and qualifiers that make no claim at all.

## Copy and marketing

- Headlines built as benefit plus product category ("The smarter way to manage X").
- Calls to action such as "Get started today" and "Discover more".
- Superlatives with no proof: best-in-class, cutting-edge, seamless, innovative, game-changing.
- Copy that could be moved to a competitor's site without changing a word.

## Fiction and scripts

- Openings that describe the weather or a character waking up.
- Characters who state their feelings in dialogue.
- Eyes that sparkle, breath that catches, silence that is deafening.
- A clean three-act arc with the lesson spoken aloud at the end.
- Endings that resolve every thread.

## Names and taglines

- Portmanteaus of two generic words, and invented words ending in -ify, -ly or -io.
- Greek or Latin roots chosen only because they sound important.
- Taglines that are a verb plus "the future of" plus a category.

## Visual and graphic design

- Centred, symmetrical compositions with the subject in the middle.
- Blue-to-purple gradients, glossy 3D blobs, isometric illustration.
- Stock-photo smiles, people pointing at screens, handshakes.
- One sans-serif typeface at three weights, with everything evenly spaced.

## Web and interface design

- A centred hero with headline, subline and two buttons, followed by three feature cards with icons.
- Testimonial carousels, logo walls and an FAQ accordion, in that order.
- Identical border radius, shadows and spacing on every element.
- Animation that fades everything in on scroll, regardless of meaning.

## Music and audio

- Four-chord loops in 4/4, with verse, chorus, verse, chorus, bridge, chorus.
- Every section exactly eight or sixteen bars long.
- Lyrics that rhyme "fire" with "desire" and "heart" with "apart".
- Perfect quantisation and constant loudness, with no silence anywhere.

## Video and presentations

- A title slide, an agenda, three bullets per slide and a "Thank you" slide.
- Drone shots, slow motion and swelling strings at the emotional beat.
- Each slide summarises itself in its title, so the talk adds nothing.

## Second-order defaults

The first clever twist is an average too: a capable model reaches the same twist run after run, and so does every other model. Treat these families as the floor once you have stepped over the plain version.

- The parody of an official document: a notice to residents, a medicine leaflet, a receipt, a delivery note, a form, a death notice. One borrowed format can work; reaching for it by reflex cannot.
- The fake apology or warning that turns a benefit into a mock complaint.
- Owning the flaw: renaming the product's weakness as its badge, or "not for everyone".
- The most familiar small mishap of the category as headline, the one every reader of the category has already seen joked about.
- Withholding the image: a black screen, sound first, the subject shown only at the end.
- Treating a website or a campaign as the object it is about (a book with an index and colophon, a menu, a ticket).

The transplant test catches most of them: if the twist would work unchanged for a competitor, another street or another product, it comes from the medium, not from the brief.

## Moves worth trying, by medium

Each move breaks one convention. Use it only when you can say what it does for the reader.

**Writing and fiction**
- Start after the climax, or cut a scene one beat before the expected resolution, so the reader supplies the feeling.
- Break a run of long sentences with a two-word one. Let dialogue trail off where a character hides something.
- Replace every abstract emotion word with an object or an action that proves it.

**Copy**
- Admit a specific limitation instead of claiming perfection.
- Give one bossy, concrete instruction ("drink it black, out of a thick mug") instead of a benefit.

**Visual design and photography**
- Push the subject to an extreme edge, or crop through the display type.
- Allow one imperfection on purpose: shooting into the light, slight motion blur, print grain next to clean vectors.
- Take the palette from a physical object instead of the category's usual colours.
- In image prompts, state light direction, lens, texture and what the viewer should notice second.

**Web and interface**
- Let the layout follow something true of this business (how its work is done, the shape of its place) instead of a template or a generic print object.
- Give empty states, errors and 404 pages candid human copy.
- Add one micro-interaction that rewards curiosity, and keep every interaction accessible by keyboard.
- Deliver a working prototype, not a description of one.

**Music and audio**
- Drop a beat at a turnaround, move the bridge into an odd meter, or delay the chorus with two bars of near silence.
- Strip the arrangement to one dry voice and one instrument before the full texture arrives.
- Use slant and internal rhymes, and phrases that spill across the bar line.
- Always state tempo, meter, key or mode, instrumentation and the dynamic arc.

## Where to look instead

Anchors come from outside the medium's usual pool of references. Start with the brief itself, because an anchor taken from it passes the transplant test by construction. Places worth trying:

- The user's own world: a fact in the brief that no competitor shares, such as their street, their hours, their process, their customers' actual words.
- Local specifics: one street's reputation, one shop sign, one dialect word, one regional habit.
- Hands and crafts: how something is folded, stitched, bound, repaired or packed.
- Obsolete technology: early animation toys, telegraph codes, punch cards, mechanical counters.
- Other art forms: the tension in a particular painting, the timing of a comedian, the structure of a recipe.
- Everyday paperwork: tickets, receipts, forms, labels. It is the most worn source; see the second-order defaults.

Prefer a specific instance over a category. "A misprinted parking ticket" is an anchor; "everyday objects" is not.
